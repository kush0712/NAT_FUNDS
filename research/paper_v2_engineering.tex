\begin{abstract}
In India, a severe data-access barrier frustrates the development of open-source mutual fund analytics. Specifically, time-series data for the BSE Total Return Index (TRI) cannot be fetched through any public, programmatic API. To circumvent this constraint within the NAT\_FUNDS platform, we designed a \emph{data-layer alias}. This mechanism seamlessly injects corresponding NSE Nifty TRI records into the caching layer wherever a BSE benchmark key is requested. Our objective here is to formally evaluate the downstream consequences of this architectural workaround.

\textbf{Critical scope limitation:} An obvious epistemological limit governs this study. We cannot directly measure proxy fidelity against BSE TRI because that exact data is missing. Instead, we rely on BSE Price Index series (sourced from Yahoo Finance) to serve as our surrogate baseline. As a result, the correlations reported below establish a strict \emph{lower bound} on the true fidelity between Nifty and BSE TRI. Price indices, by definition, fail to capture dividend reinvestment.

Testing across 120 months of returns (July 2016--June 2026, $n=120$) reveals that the Pearson correlation linking BSE Price indices with Nifty TRI proxies sits tightly between $r=0.965$ and $r=0.975$ for broad-market pairings (Spearman $\rho=0.951$--$0.964$). Examining a convenience sample comprised of 13 large-cap ETFs, we found a mean $\Delta\beta$ of $+0.025$ ($SD=0.014$). In practical retail applications, this shift is economically trivial. We also measured a mean $\Delta\alpha$ of $-1.77\%$ ($SD=0.53\%$). Rather than proving proxy superiority, this alpha shift merely confirms a well-documented accounting identity: the prevailing Indian equity dividend yield rests near $1.5$--$2.0\%$ annually. Furthermore, rolling-window robustness checks demonstrate that while the absolute magnitude of $\Delta\beta$ stays contained ($\approx0.025$--$0.090$), the actual sign flips depending on the historical epoch examined. Finally, we highlight a severe failure mode. When structurally non-equivalent indices are blindly paired---such as substituting Nifty CPSE for BSE Select Business Groups ($r=0.656$)---the correlation collapses, underscoring the absolute necessity for curated engineering guardrails.
\end{abstract}

%% ─── I. Introduction ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

While open-source analytics platforms have radically democratized access to retail investment data, developers operating within emerging markets still grapple with crippling data asymmetries. Institutional actors effortlessly pull pristine index histories from commercial terminals like Refinitiv or Bloomberg. Conversely, retail and open-source developers find the exact same datasets locked behind paywalls, disrupted by aggressive session gating, or entirely deprecated \cite{milan2020}.

In the Indian context, a 2018 regulatory decree drastically compounded this asymmetry. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) mandated that every mutual fund scheme must measure its performance against Total Return Indices (TRI), officially abandoning the older Price indices \cite{sebi2018}. The logic was sound. Because TRI series fold dividend payouts back into the calculation, they capture true economic returns---something Price indices systematically fail to do. Thus, utilizing TRI became a hard regulatory baseline. Yet, bizarrely, BSE TRI data remained virtually impossible to access via programmatic channels.

The National Stock Exchange (NSE) offers a reasonably robust Nifty Indices API, providing documented endpoints that serve complete TRI histories. The BSE chose a different path. They actively deprecated their bulk TRI download endpoints and never deployed a public JSON or REST alternative. Faced with this void, open-source engineers attempting to evaluate BSE-benchmarked funds are forced into an unpleasant corner. They must either drop those funds from their platforms, revert to the non-compliant Price indices, or attempt to hack a solution by swapping in a closely related NSE series.

In constructing NAT\_FUNDS, we opted for the third approach. We engineered what we describe as a \emph{data-layer alias}. Specifically, we map incoming Nifty TRI data arrays into our JSON cache using BSE benchmark identifiers. The primary aim of this paper is to rigorously quantify the downstream distortions this substitution inflicts on calculated risk metrics (namely, Beta and Alpha). We offer four main contributions: \textbf{(1)} a verifiable, reproducible methodology for testing proxy fidelity when the underlying target data is missing; \textbf{(2)} precise metric error measurements across 13 distinct large-cap ETFs; \textbf{(3)} the discovery of a critical failure state triggered by pairing structurally disparate indices; and \textbf{(4)} a transparent accounting of the epistemological constraints limiting our own findings.

%% ─── II. Related Work ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Tracking Error and Benchmark Fidelity}
Academics have long scrutinized how closely tracking instruments adhere to their stated benchmarks, primarily within mature Western markets. Rompotis \cite{rompotis2011}, for example, outlines highly predictable patterns governing ETF tracking errors, linking them to underlying liquidity constraints, fee structures, and the chosen replication mechanics. Notice, however, that this entire stream of literature examines deviations between a fund and its benchmark. Our work probes a fundamentally distinct---and largely ignored---phenomenon: the benchmark-to-benchmark deviation artificially generated by data substitution deep inside a system's architecture.

\subsection{Data Accessibility and Emerging Market Fintech}
In their critical analysis of technology research, Milan and Treré \cite{milan2020} coin the term \emph{data universalism} to describe the flawed, implicit belief that data infrastructure operates identically across the globe. The sudden deprecation of the BSE API exemplifies this exact bias. By locking away data, it imposes a severe structural handicap on independent open-source developers while leaving well-funded institutional participants untouched. Engineers inevitably respond by cobbling together workarounds, yet the statistical fallout of these hacks is almost never documented. As far as our literature review reveals, no previous study has quantified the metric-level impact of infrastructure substitutions within the Indian mutual fund sector.

\subsection{Regulatory Context: SEBI's TRI Mandate}
The 2018 SEBI circular \cite{sebi2018} enforcing TRI compliance is what elevates this issue from a mere technical annoyance to a regulatory problem. Calculating Alpha against a standard Price index inevitably inflates the perceived outperformance of a fund by an amount roughly equal to the market dividend yield (historically around $1.5$--$2.0\%$ annually in India). Once SEBI codified the TRI standard, securing accurate total return data morphed into a mandatory, compliance-adjacent requirement for anyone building retail analytics platforms.

%% ─── III. System Architecture ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Architecture: The Data-Layer Alias}

Early in the development of NAT\_FUNDS, the deprecation of the BSE API forced a major design choice. We could have injected conditional proxy logic directly into the metric computation modules. Doing so, however, would have riddled the codebase with fragile, per-fund branching statements. We rejected that approach, opting instead to execute the substitution silently at the data-persistence layer.

Within our JSON cache, Nifty TRI payloads are saved using BSE benchmark keys. If the application queries the backend for \texttt{BSE SENSEX TRI}, the database simply hands back the daily NAV array belonging to \texttt{Nifty 50 TRI}. The same logic maps \texttt{BSE 100 TRI} to Nifty 100 TRI, continuing down the list. The overriding benefit here is strict architectural uniformity. Downstream mathematical functions calculating Beta, Alpha, or Sharpe ratios utilize identical, untouched code paths. The math layer is entirely blind to the substitution.

This elegance comes at a steep perceptual cost. Because the aliasing happens invisibly in the background, a normal user has no way of knowing that the BSE metrics they see are actually driven by NSE Nifty data. Recognizing the potential for retail harm, we subsequently pushed an update to NAT\_FUNDS that renders a clear inline tooltip on every affected fund page, explicitly outlining the substitution. Our primary architectural recommendation is unequivocal: any system deploying a data-layer alias must pair it with front-end UI disclosures to ensure users are not misled.

%% ─── IV. Empirical Methodology ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Empirical Methodology}

We structured our empirical testing into four discrete stages, moving progressively from raw baseline correlations to complex rolling-window validations.

\subsection{Stage 0: Architectural Discovery and Index Correlation}
\label{sec:stage0}
A stark limitation dictates our methodology: the exact variable we wish to measure (BSE TRI) is precisely the data we cannot obtain. To circumvent this, we extracted public BSE Price Index histories from Yahoo Finance (\texttt{\^{}BSESN}, \texttt{BSE-100.BO}, \texttt{BSE-200.BO}, \texttt{BSE-500.BO}) to act as an independent, surrogate baseline. It is crucial to internalize that all error margins and correlations in this study are computed against BSE Price, not BSE TRI. Given that Price series shed dividend reinvestments, the actual hidden correlation between Nifty and BSE TRI is almost certainly \emph{stronger} than the baseline figures we report.

We analyzed a 120-month window of monthly returns (July 2016--June 2026, $n=120$). This timeframe was rigidly constrained by the earliest date Yahoo Finance provided unbroken BSE series data. We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients, subsequently generating 95\% confidence intervals via 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples using the percentile method (anchored by NumPy's \texttt{default\_rng} with seed=42). To guard against outlier distortion, we also ran Spearman rank correlations as a secondary, non-parametric check.

\subsection{Stage 1: Metric-Level Error Quantification}
\label{sec:stage1}
Seeking to quantify real-world metric distortions, we isolated a convenience sample of 13 BSE-benchmarked mutual fund ETFs directly from the active NAT\_FUNDS cache (see Appendix~\ref{app:funds} for exact scheme names). For every fund, we synchronized 36 contiguous months of returns across three distinct vectors: the raw Fund NAV, our cached Proxy Benchmark (Nifty TRI), and the True Baseline (Yahoo Finance BSE Price). 

We calculated the standard three-year Beta via $\hat{\beta} = \text{Cov}(r_f, r_b) / \text{Var}(r_b)$. Alpha was derived monthly as $\alpha_\text{monthly} = \bar{r}_f - r_f^0 - \hat{\beta}(\bar{r}_b - r_f^0)$, utilizing an assumed risk-free rate $r_f^0 = 6.0\%$ p.a.\ (mirroring the approximate yield of Indian 91-day T-bills), and then annualized via $(1 + \alpha_\text{monthly})^{12} - 1$. We tracked the specific deviations using $\Delta\beta = \beta_\text{proxy} - \beta_\text{true}$ and $\Delta\alpha = \alpha_\text{proxy} - \alpha_\text{true}$, deploying a two-tailed paired $t$-test to verify statistical significance.

\subsection{Stage 2: Statistical Validation (Rolling-Window Robustness)}
\label{sec:stage2}
A static 36-month snapshot rarely tells the whole story. To confirm longitudinal stability, we executed a rolling-window analysis spanning the entire July 2016--June 2026 dataset. By advancing a 36-month calculation frame one month at a time across all 13 funds, we generated 849 distinct (fund, window) observations for both $\Delta\beta$ and $\Delta\alpha$. Calculating the aggregate mean, standard deviation, and inter-quartile range (IQR) over these rolling blocks provides a far more rigorous measure of the proxy's temporal reliability.

\subsection{Stage 3: Synthesis and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:stage3}
Ultimately, we synthesized the preceding stages into a cohesive assessment of proxy viability. Anyone wishing to audit our math can find the core application codebase at the repository root (\url{https://github.com/kush0712/NAT_FUNDS}). All analytical Python scripts, raw data files, and output matrices are quarantined inside the \texttt{research/} sub-directory. We bifurcated the scripts: \texttt{phase0/} handles the raw correlation math, while \texttt{phase1/} computes the metric errors and executes the rolling windows. Both directories contain isolated \texttt{data/} and \texttt{outputs/} folders to prevent contamination. Pinned dependencies sit in \texttt{research/requirements.txt}, and a comprehensive replication guide is available at \texttt{research/README.md}.

\subsection{Broad Market Correlation}
Unsurprisingly, broad-market BSE Price indices demonstrate intense co-movement with our Nifty TRI proxies (see Table~\ref{tab:correlation}). Across four major index pairs, the Pearson $r$ locked in tightly between $0.965$ and $0.975$. Furthermore, the corresponding Spearman $\rho$ metrics never deviated by more than $0.013$ from the Pearson baselines, providing strong evidence that the high correlations are not merely artifacts of a few stray outliers. Every single 95\% bootstrap confidence interval maintained a minimum bound above $0.934$.

\subsection{Thematic Divergence Risk}
Attempting to substitute indices that lack strict structural equivalence reliably produces degraded correlations (Table~\ref{tab:thematic}). When we tested the Nifty CPSE index (dominated by government-owned Public Sector Enterprises) as a stand-in for the BSE Select Business Groups index (which tracks family-controlled conglomerates), the Pearson correlation plummeted to $r=0.656$ ($\rho=0.674$). This drop is not a computational glitch. It reflects severe compositional divergence. Likewise, matching the Nifty 200 Quality 30 against the BSE Quality TRI only achieves $r=0.889$, hampered by fundamentally mismatched factor weightings and sector exposures. The implication is severe: deploying automated, wholesale proxy mapping without manually verifying constituent-level parity introduces massive engineering risks.

\subsection{Beta Stability}
Within our 13-fund convenience sample, applying the proxy forces a mean shift of $\Delta\beta=+0.025$ ($SD=0.014$). We confirmed the statistical reality of this shift via a two-tailed paired $t$-test, which returned $t(12)=6.24$, $p<0.001$. However, statistical significance does not imply economic relevance. An absolute Beta deviation of $0.025$ is virtually undetectable in standard retail analytics. Assuming a baseline market return of $12\%$ annually, this error skews total performance attribution by a mere $0.30\%$. Viewed strictly through a fitness-for-purpose lens, the Nifty proxy successfully preserves Beta integrity for major broad-market funds.

\subsection{Proxy vs.\ BSE Price Alpha Differential}
Our tests recorded a mean $\Delta\alpha$ of $-1.77\%$ ($SD=0.53\%$). Rather than acting as an independent stamp of approval for proxy quality, this metric is best understood as a \emph{mechanical confirmation of a standard accounting identity}. Because BSE Price indices strip out dividend reinvestments, analyzing a fund against them will automatically inflate the manager's apparent Alpha by roughly the aggregate market dividend yield. Indian equities historically maintain a dividend yield oscillating between $1.5\%$ and $2.0\%$ annually. This reality entirely explains the observed shift. The math holds perfectly: $\Delta\alpha \approx -(\text{dividend yield}) \times 1 \approx -1.77\%$. 

Consequently, utilizing the Nifty TRI proxy successfully neutralizes this dangerous Price-index bias, yielding a decidedly more honest assessment of managerial skill. What this metric fails to prove, however, is whether Nifty TRI perfectly mimics the \emph{specific} behavior of BSE TRI. We simply lack the necessary proprietary data to answer that granular question.

\subsection{Rolling-Window Robustness}
Expanding our view to the rolling-window analysis (encompassing 849 distinct fund-window observations, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}) reveals a crucial caveat: the static 36-month snapshot is \emph{not} representative of long-term distributions. 

Analyzing every overlapping 36-month slice between July 2016 and June 2026 yields a mean $\Delta\beta$ of $-0.088$ ($SD=0.046$; IQR $[-0.112, -0.064]$). Note the negative sign. Our static test, which isolated only the most recent 36 months, produced a positive shift of $+0.025$. This inversion is highly instructive. It demonstrates that the relative volatility differential between NSE and BSE indices mutates over time. Consequently, the directional bias imposed by the proxy is not historically stable, though its absolute magnitude reliably stays contained within a tight corridor ($|\Delta\beta| \approx 0.025$--$0.090$).

Similarly, the aggregate mean $\Delta\alpha$ across all windows landed at $-0.51\%$ ($SD=1.19\%$; IQR $[-1.27\%, +0.12\%]$). The expansive IQR points to extreme temporal variation. Depending on the exact historical window analyzed, the proxy might severely depress apparent Alpha, or conversely, slightly elevate it. This volatility aligns perfectly with the year-to-year fluctuations inherent to Indian equity dividend yields, compounded by shifting performance dominance between NSE and BSE segments. 

Ultimately, these longitudinal checks validate the epistemological threats discussed below. Anyone deploying this proxy methodology must recognize that static Beta errors derived from recent history cannot be safely extrapolated backward.

%% ─── VI. Threats to Validity ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\subsection{Self-Evaluation (Internal Validity)}
The most acute vulnerability in this study lies in the fact that we are evaluating our own engineering architecture. To mitigate this risk, we deliberately pre-authored all analysis scripts prior to tabulating the final output matrices, and we rigorously documented clear failure cases (as seen in Section~V-B). Nevertheless, the specter of unconscious confirmation bias---affecting everything from baseline selection to rhetorical framing---remains a valid concern. Achieving definitive proof would necessitate an independent audit conducted by external researchers utilizing paid commercial BSE TRI feeds.

\subsection{Convenience Sample and Selection Bias (External Validity)}
We extracted our 13-fund sample straight from the NAT\_FUNDS caching layer. Because the platform natively utilizes the proxy substitution under review, the sample is inherently skewed toward funds that process without catastrophic error. Specifically, large-cap BSE SENSEX ETFs managed by dominant Asset Management Companies (AMCs) dominate the list. These specific instruments typically exhibit the highest possible benchmark fidelity. Consequently, our findings cannot be safely generalized to mid-cap, small-cap, or highly niche thematic funds. Additionally, we did not execute a formal statistical power analysis to validate $n=13$.

\subsection{Proxy-vs-Proxy Comparison (Construct Validity)}
An intractable epistemological limit defines this research: the exact data series we wish to benchmark against (BSE TRI) is precisely the series whose total inaccessibility prompted our engineering workaround. Every statistical correlation and metric error documented in this paper relies on BSE Price as a baseline surrogate. As a direct result, our calculations successfully define a \emph{lower bound} on proxy fidelity, but they offer zero insight into the upper bound. The true tracking accuracy of Nifty TRI relative to BSE TRI might be vastly superior---or notably worse---than the figures we measured. Without access to the proprietary target data, that exact differential remains mathematically unknowable.

\subsection{Single Time Window (Temporal Validity)}
Our dataset is confined to the decade spanning July 2016 through June 2026. While the rolling-window analysis in Section~V-E confirms a degree of internal stability, this ten-year block essentially encompasses one massive, sustained bull market (punctuated only briefly by the 2020 COVID contraction and the 2022 rate shocks). We have not tested---and cannot guarantee---the integrity of the proxy relationship during prolonged, structural market regime shifts or localized BSE/NSE liquidity crises.

%% ─── VII. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

Deploying a data-layer alias to blindly substitute Nifty TRI for the inaccessible BSE TRI within the NAT\_FUNDS ecosystem inevitably forces a statistically distinct, yet economically trivial, Beta shift during recent 36-month intervals ($\Delta\beta\approx+0.025$). Crucially, this substitution effectively dodges the massive dividend accounting distortions inherent to the only available public alternative (BSE Price indices). We also identified a major operational failure mode: blindly substituting structurally disparate thematic indices (such as utilizing Nifty CPSE for BSE Select Business Groups, yielding $r=0.656$). Mitigating this risk requires strict, hand-curated engineering guardrails validated down to the constituent level.

Furthermore, longitudinal rolling-window checks prove that the directional sign of the Beta error is historically unstable. Across the entire 2016--2026 dataset, the mean shift was distinctly negative ($-0.088$, IQR $[-0.112, -0.064]$), directly contradicting the positive shift observed in the most recent static window. This confirms that proxy-induced distortion is highly sensitive to the specific epoch being measured and cannot be blindly generalized as a static constant.

Four stark limitations constrain our findings: (1) we evaluate a proxy against a price index rather than the true target; (2) our fund sample relies on convenience sampling from our native architecture; (3) the study constitutes an inherent self-audit; and (4) short-term static windows misrepresent long-term historical variance. Consequently, these results should be strictly interpreted as \emph{engineering necessity bounds}. The proxy functions adequately within the measurable parameters, but the ultimate question of precise, granular tracking fidelity remains entirely unresolved without commercial BSE TRI feeds.

For developers forced to adopt similar data-layer workarounds, our primary architectural mandate is uncompromising front-end transparency. Any risk metric derived from a proxy benchmark must be explicitly disclosed via the user interface, complete with documented correlation parameters.
