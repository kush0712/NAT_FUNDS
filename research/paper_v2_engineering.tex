\begin{abstract}
If you build open-source tools for mutual fund analytics in India, you quickly hit a major roadblock: there simply isn't a public, programmatic API for fetching BSE Total Return Index (TRI) time-series data. In building the NAT\_FUNDS platform, we bypassed this issue by introducing a \emph{data-layer alias}. Basically, whenever the system needs a BSE benchmark, it silently swaps in the equivalent NSE Nifty TRI series right at the caching layer. In this paper, we take a hard look at whether that architectural shortcut holds up.

\textbf{Critical scope limitation:} We have to point out a fundamental catch right away. Because the actual BSE TRI data is locked away, we can't directly compare our proxy against it. Instead, we had to rely on the publicly available BSE Price Index data from Yahoo Finance as a stand-in baseline. Since Price indices don't factor in dividend reinvestment, the correlations we report here act as a strict \emph{lower bound} on the real Nifty-to-BSE-TRI fidelity.

Looking at 120 months of data (July 2016--June 2026, $n=120$), the Pearson correlation between the BSE Price indices and our Nifty TRI proxies fell between $r=0.965$ and $r=0.975$ for broad-market pairs, with Spearman $\rho$ hitting $0.951$--$0.964$. We also pulled a convenience sample of 13 large-cap ETFs, where we saw a mean $\Delta\beta$ of $+0.025$ ($SD=0.014$)---a shift small enough to ignore for everyday retail use. We did see a mean $\Delta\alpha$ of $-1.77\%$ ($SD=0.53\%$), but that's entirely expected; it perfectly matches the typical Indian equity dividend yield ($\approx1.5$--$2.0\%$ p.a.) and shouldn't be taken as independent proof that the proxy matches BSE TRI. Our rolling-window checks showed that while $|\Delta\beta|$ stays well bounded in magnitude over time ($\approx0.025$--$0.090$), the actual sign flips depending on whether you look at recent years or earlier ones. Finally, mismatching index themes---like trying to swap Nifty CPSE for BSE Select Business Groups ($r=0.656$)---is a surefire way to break things, meaning developers need to put strict guardrails in place to avoid naive substitutions.
\end{abstract}

%% ─── I. Introduction ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

There's no doubt that the rise of open-source tools for retail investors has opened up access to financial data like never before. But if you're actually trying to build these systems in emerging markets, you run into some brutal data asymmetries. The pristine index data that big institutions grab easily through expensive terminals like Bloomberg or Refinitiv is usually locked behind paywalls, blocked by session gates, or just completely shut off for retail developers \cite{milan2020}.

In India, this data gap took on a very specific shape back in 2018, thanks to a regulatory push. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) laid down a rule that mutual funds had to start benchmarking against Total Return Indices (TRI) instead of the old Price indices \cite{sebi2018}. It makes total sense---TRI includes reinvested dividends, so it captures the actual economic return, whereas Price indices artificially drag down the benchmark's performance. The problem? Even though TRI benchmarking became mandatory, the programmatic access to BSE TRI data remained practically non-existent.

While the NSE stepped up and offered a working Nifty Indices API that gives you full TRI history, the BSE went the other way. They ended up deprecating their bulk download endpoints for TRI data and never bothered to offer a public REST or JSON API to replace it. This leaves open-source developers stuck between a rock and a hard place when trying to analyze BSE-benchmarked funds. You basically have three bad choices: skip those funds altogether, go back to using the SEBI-rejected Price indices, or try to hack it by using a similar NSE series as a stand-in.

For the NAT\_FUNDS platform, we took that third route. We built what we call a \emph{data-layer alias}---essentially taking Nifty TRI values and filing them away under BSE benchmark names right in our JSON cache. The goal of this paper is to figure out exactly how much error this shortcut injects into downstream risk metrics like Beta and Alpha. Our main contributions here are: \textbf{(1)} laying out a reproducible way to test proxy fidelity when the target data is missing in action; \textbf{(2)} putting hard numbers on the metric errors for a group of 13 large-cap ETFs; \textbf{(3)} flagging a major failure mode that happens when you try to pair up indices that don't share the same thematic DNA; and \textbf{(4)} being brutally honest about the limits of what we can actually prove.

%% ─── II. Related Work ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Tracking Error and Benchmark Fidelity}
People have spent plenty of time studying how well index-tracking instruments actually follow their benchmarks, especially in developed markets. For instance, Rompotis \cite{rompotis2011} highlighted that ETF tracking errors aren't totally random; they're driven by things like liquidity, how much the fund charges in fees, and the specific ways they try to replicate the index. But nearly all of that research looks at how a fund deviates from its benchmark. We are looking at a completely different beast: the error introduced when you swap one benchmark for another deep inside the system's infrastructure.

\subsection{Data Accessibility and Emerging Market Fintech}
In their work on technology research biases, Milan and Treré \cite{milan2020} point out a concept called \emph{data universalism}---the flawed idea that everyone globally has access to the same quality of data infrastructure. The whole situation with the BSE API shutting down is a textbook example of this. It tilts the playing field heavily against open-source developers while institutional players carry on as usual. Developers are forced to cobble together workarounds, and nobody really talks about how those hacks warp the final numbers. As far as we can tell, no one has actually sat down and measured the fallout of these data substitutions in the Indian mutual fund space.

\subsection{Regulatory Context: SEBI's TRI Mandate}
SEBI's 2018 ruling that mandated TRI-based benchmarking \cite{sebi2018} is the whole reason this headache exists. If you calculate Alpha or general returns against a standard Price index, you end up artificially inflating how well the fund performed by roughly the market's dividend yield (which hovers around $1.5$--$2.0\%$ a year for Indian equities). Once SEBI laid down that rule, grabbing accurate TRI data wasn't just a nice-to-have; it became a hard engineering requirement if you wanted your retail platform to stay honest.

%% ─── III. System Architecture ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Architecture: The Data-Layer Alias}

When we were getting NAT\_FUNDS off the ground, hitting the wall with the deprecated BSE API forced our hand. We could have written messy, conditional logic right where the metrics get calculated, but that would have polluted the whole codebase. Instead, we decided to handle the swap quietly at the data-persistence layer.

Here is how it works: we take Nifty TRI values and deliberately save them under BSE benchmark names inside our JSON cache. So, if the system asks for the \texttt{BSE SENSEX TRI}, it gets handed the exact same array of daily NAVs as \texttt{Nifty 50 TRI}. The same goes for \texttt{BSE 100 TRI} mapping to Nifty 100 TRI, and the rest. The big win here is architectural consistency. Every piece of math that runs downstream---whether it's Alpha, Beta, Sharpe, or Sortino---just processes the data exactly the same way, blissfully unaware that a swap even happened.

But this clever trick comes with a serious catch. Since the switch happens invisibly in the background, regular users have no idea that the BSE metrics they are looking at are actually powered by Nifty data. Once we saw the results of this study, we realized we had to fix that. We ended up pushing an update to NAT\_FUNDS that adds a clear UI tooltip on the affected fund pages, making sure retail investors know exactly what's going on. The big takeaway here for any engineer trying to pull a similar stunt is simple: if you are going to alias data like this, you absolutely have to disclose it to the user so you don't end up misleading them.

%% ─── IV. Empirical Methodology ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Empirical Methodology}

We broke down our testing into four clear stages, starting from just figuring out what was happening under the hood, all the way to rigorous statistical checks.

\subsection{Stage 0: Architectural Discovery and Index Correlation}
\label{sec:stage0}
Here is the tricky part: we can't actually measure the exact error between Nifty TRI and BSE TRI because, well, the BSE TRI data is exactly what we can't access. To get around this, we pulled BSE Price Index data straight from Yahoo Finance (\texttt{\^{}BSESN}, \texttt{BSE-100.BO}, \texttt{BSE-200.BO}, \texttt{BSE-500.BO}) to serve as an independent baseline. These are pretty much the only long-term BSE datasets you can grab for free. So, it's important to remember that all the correlations and errors we talk about are measured against BSE Price, not BSE TRI. Because Price indices miss out on dividend reinvestment, it's highly likely that the real Nifty-to-BSE-TRI correlation is actually \emph{better} than the numbers we found.

Our data covers 120 months of returns (from July 2016 to June 2026, $n=120$), pinned to the earliest date we could get a solid, unbroken run of BSE index data on Yahoo Finance. We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients and used 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples (with the percentile method, leaning on NumPy's \texttt{default\_rng} with seed=42) to lock in 95\% confidence intervals. Just to be safe, we also ran Spearman rank correlations as a non-parametric sanity check.

\subsection{Stage 1: Metric-Level Error Quantification}
\label{sec:stage1}
To see how this swap actually messes with the numbers, we grabbed a convenience sample of 13 mutual fund ETFs that benchmark against the BSE, pulling them right from NAT\_FUNDS' live cache (you can find the full list of funds in Appendix~\ref{app:funds}). For every single fund, we lined up 36 months of returns across three different series: the Fund NAV, the Proxy Benchmark (our cached Nifty TRI), and the True Baseline (the Yahoo Finance BSE Price data). 

We calculated the three-year Beta as $\hat{\beta} = \text{Cov}(r_f, r_b) / \text{Var}(r_b)$. For Alpha, we annualized it using $(1 + \alpha_\text{monthly})^{12} - 1$, where the monthly alpha was derived as $\alpha_\text{monthly} = \bar{r}_f - r_f^0 - \hat{\beta}(\bar{r}_b - r_f^0)$, assuming a risk-free rate of $6.0\%$ p.a.\ (which roughly matches the yield on Indian 91-day T-bills over this time, compounded monthly). We tracked the exact error margins as $\Delta\beta = \beta_\text{proxy} - \beta_\text{true}$ and $\Delta\alpha = \alpha_\text{proxy} - \alpha_\text{true}$, then ran a two-tailed paired $t$-test to see if the shifts were statistically meaningful.

\subsection{Stage 2: Statistical Validation (Rolling-Window Robustness)}
\label{sec:stage2}
We didn't want to just trust a single 36-month snapshot, so we ran a rolling-window analysis to see if the numbers held up over time. We calculated $\Delta\beta$ and $\Delta\alpha$ across every possible contiguous 36-month block between July 2016 and June 2026, inching forward one month at a time. Across our 13 funds, that gave us 849 distinct data points. Looking at the mean, standard deviation, and inter-quartile range (IQR) over all those windows gave us a much better feel for whether the proxy's behavior was actually stable.

\subsection{Stage 3: Synthesis and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:stage3}
The last step was pulling all these pieces together into one cohesive look at how well the proxy performs. If you want to poke around the core code yourself, it's all sitting in the project repository at \url{https://github.com/kush0712/NAT_FUNDS}. We've neatly quarantined all the Python scripts, raw CSVs, and output data into a dedicated \texttt{research/} folder. Inside that, everything is split up logically: \texttt{phase0/} handles the index correlations, while \texttt{phase1/} takes care of the metric errors and the rolling-window tests. Each folder has its own \texttt{data/} and \texttt{outputs/} subdirectories. We also made sure to pin the exact dependencies in \texttt{research/requirements.txt} and wrote up a clear, step-by-step guide in \texttt{research/README.md} so anyone can reproduce the exact numbers we got.

%% ─── V. Results & Discussion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Results \& Discussion}

\subsection{Broad Market Correlation}
It turns out that broad-market BSE Price indices move almost in lockstep with our Nifty TRI proxies (see Table~\ref{tab:correlation}). Looking across the four broad-market pairs, the Pearson $r$ landed tightly between $0.965$ and $0.975$. The Spearman $\rho$ values were also right in the same neighborhood, never drifting more than $0.013$ away from the Pearson numbers, which tells us that a few stray outlier months aren't skewing the results. Plus, all of our 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals safely cleared a minimum bound of $0.934$.

Just to re-emphasize what we mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:stage0}: these correlations are just a floor. The actual BSE TRI series would include dividend reinvestment, meaning it should track the Nifty TRI even more closely than the raw Price indices do.

\subsection{Thematic Divergence Risk}
Things get messy quickly if you try to substitute indices that aren't structurally identical (Table~\ref{tab:thematic}). For example, trying to swap the Nifty CPSE index (which focuses on government-owned public sector enterprises) for the BSE Select Business Groups index (which tracks family-controlled conglomerates) gives a pretty dismal correlation of $r=0.656$ and $\rho=0.674$. This isn't just a data glitch; these indices genuinely behave differently. Similarly, matching the quality-factor weighted Nifty 200 Quality 30 against the BSE Quality TRI only gets you up to $r=0.889$. The takeaway is clear: if you try to blindly automate proxy mapping across all BSE indices without actually checking if the underlying constituents match up, you're asking for engineering trouble.

\subsection{Beta Stability}
When we looked at our convenience sample of 13 funds, the proxy caused a mean shift of $\Delta\beta=+0.025$ ($SD=0.014$). We ran a two-tailed paired $t$-test and got $t(12)=6.24$, $p<0.001$, which proves the shift is statistically real. But practically speaking? An absolute shift of just $0.025$ in Beta is virtually meaningless for a retail investor trying to evaluate a fund. If the market returns $12\%$ in a year, a Beta error this small only skews the fund's expected performance by about $0.30\%$. So, for broad-market funds, the proxy does a perfectly fine job of keeping Beta accurate enough for its intended purpose.

\subsection{Proxy vs.\ BSE Price Alpha Differential}
We saw a mean $\Delta\alpha$ of $-1.77\%$ ($SD=0.53\%$). Honestly, this number is better viewed as \emph{a neat proof of a well-known accounting rule} rather than an independent gold star for the proxy's quality.

Since BSE Price indices don't reinvest dividends, calculating a fund's Alpha against them artificially pumps up the manager's apparent skill by roughly whatever the market's dividend yield is. Historically, Indian equities pay out around $1.5$--$2.0\%$ a year in dividends, which perfectly explains the $\Delta\alpha$ we're seeing. It basically boils down to $\Delta\alpha \approx -(\text{dividend yield}) \times 1 \approx -1.77\%$, which lines up exactly with standard index math.

What this result really proves is that switching to the Nifty TRI proxy successfully \emph{strips out} this misleading Price-index bias for the 13 funds we tested, which is definitely a win. An Alpha score calculated against Nifty TRI gives you a much truer picture of manager skill than one measured against a stripped-down BSE Price benchmark. What it doesn't prove, unfortunately, is whether Nifty TRI is a flawless proxy for the real BSE TRI, because we still don't have the data to make that direct comparison.

\subsection{Rolling-Window Robustness}
When we expanded our view and ran the rolling-window analysis (totaling 849 distinct fund/window observations, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:stage2}), it became pretty clear that you can't just trust the single static 36-month snapshot as the whole truth.

If you look across every single 36-month slice from July 2016 through June 2026, the mean $\Delta\beta$ is actually $-0.088$ ($SD=0.046$; IQR $[-0.112, -0.064]$). Notice the negative sign? Our static test (which just looked at the most recent 36 months) showed a shift of $+0.025$. This flip in the sign is a really interesting finding on its own. It shows that the relative volatility between the BSE and NSE indices actually changes over time. The proxy doesn't just push Beta cleanly in one direction forever; it skewed positive recently, but trended negative in older periods. The good news is that the sheer size of the shift stays contained ($|\Delta\beta| \approx 0.025$--$0.090$) regardless of the era.

As for Alpha, the mean $\Delta\alpha$ across all windows settled at $-0.51\%$ ($SD=1.19\%$; IQR $[-1.27\%, +0.12\%]$). That incredibly wide IQR tells us that the Alpha gap swings wildly depending on the timeframe. During certain stretches, using the proxy actually makes a fund's Alpha look better, while in other stretches, it drags it down. This makes total sense when you factor in how the Indian dividend yield bounces around year to year, and how BSE and NSE market segments occasionally trade blows on performance.

Ultimately, these rolling-window checks really reinforce the caveats we talk about in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. The static numbers we reported in Sections~V-C and V-D are just a reflection of recent history, which might not be the norm. Anyone looking to use this proxy trick in their own work definitely needs to remember that the direction of the Beta error isn't set in stone.

%% ─── VI. Threats to Validity ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\subsection{Self-Evaluation (Internal Validity)}
The biggest elephant in the room is that we are grading our own homework here. We tried to keep things honest by writing out the analysis scripts before we actually crunched the final numbers, and we didn't shy away from reporting the failure cases (like in Section~V-B). But at the end of the day, it's impossible to completely rule out unconscious confirmation bias when we're the ones picking the metrics, choosing the baselines, and framing the results. Having an independent team run this same evaluation—ideally with access to expensive commercial BSE TRI feeds—would give these findings a lot more weight.

\subsection{Convenience Sample and Selection Bias (External Validity)}
We hand-picked the 13 funds straight from the NAT\_FUNDS live cache, which obviously already uses the proxy trick we're evaluating. That inherently skews the sample toward funds that the platform already processes smoothly. Plus, our sample is heavily tilted toward large-cap BSE SENSEX ETFs managed by major firms, which are exactly the kinds of funds you'd expect to track their benchmarks the tightest. We didn't even look at mid-cap, small-cap, or niche sector funds, so their results might look totally different. And just to be clear, we didn't do any formal power analysis to justify stopping at $n=13$.

\subsection{Proxy-vs-Proxy Comparison (Construct Validity)}
The hard truth of this study is an epistemological one: the exact thing we want to measure (BSE TRI) is the exact thing we are locked out of. Every single correlation and error metric we've reported is measured against BSE Price, not the actual BSE TRI. This means we've successfully proven a \emph{lower bound} on how well the proxy works, but we have absolutely no idea what the upper bound is. The real Nifty-to-BSE-TRI fidelity might be way better, or it might be worse; we just can't measure it with the public data we have.

\subsection{Single Time Window (Temporal Validity)}
Our data only spans from July 2016 to June 2026. Yes, the rolling-window checks (Section~V-E) suggest that the proxy holds up reasonably well across different sub-periods, but the entire decade was basically one long, sustained bull market (ignoring the brief COVID crash in 2020 and the rate shocks of 2022). We really don't know how this proxy trick would behave during a massive structural shift in the market or if liquidity between the BSE and NSE were to suddenly diverge.

%% ─── VII. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

The data-layer alias we baked into NAT\_FUNDS (swapping Nifty TRI in for the missing BSE TRI) definitely introduces a slight Beta shift in the most recent three-year window ($\Delta\beta\approx+0.025$). It is statistically real, but economically tiny. More importantly, it successfully sidesteps the massive dividend accounting error that you'd get if you resorted to using the only available BSE alternative (Price indices). Just be careful not to blindly match up indices that aren't structurally twins---trying to substitute Nifty CPSE for BSE Select Business Groups ($r=0.656$) is a great example of a failure mode that requires strict, hand-curated engineering guardrails.

That said, our rolling-window analysis makes it clear that you can't assume the Beta error will always be positive. Looking over the full 2016--2026 stretch, the mean shift was actually negative ($-0.088$, IQR $[-0.112, -0.064]$). This proves that the proxy's behavior is pretty sensitive to whatever specific timeframe you're looking at, and you shouldn't treat the error margin like a static constant.

There are four big asterisks to keep in mind here: (1) we're comparing a proxy to a price index, not the actual target; (2) we used a convenience sample pulled from our own system; (3) this is effectively a self-audit; and (4) the static time window doesn't tell the whole historical story. Ultimately, you should look at these findings as \emph{engineering necessity bounds}. The workaround functions within an acceptable margin of error based on what we can actually test, but until someone hands over the real commercial BSE TRI data, the question of whether Nifty TRI is a perfect match remains unresolved.

If you are an engineer planning to use this same substitution trick, our strongest piece of advice is to be completely transparent with your users. If you are calculating a metric against a substituted benchmark, put a clear UI note right there on the screen, and make sure you document exactly how well the substitute actually correlates.
